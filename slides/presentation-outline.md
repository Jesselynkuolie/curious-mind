
# Curious Mind — AI Overview
## Presentation Outline

**Project:** Curious Mind — AI Overview  
**Presentation title:** Understanding Artificial Intelligence: Opportunities, Limitations, and Responsible Use  
**Target audience:** Students and beginners with limited prior knowledge of AI  
**Estimated duration:** 8–10 minutes  
**Format:** 12-slide educational presentation  
**Related materials:** Module outline, learning chapters, study guide, practice questions, and mini-lecture script

---

## 1. Presentation Purpose

This presentation introduces the fundamental concepts of artificial intelligence (AI), explains how machine learning and generative AI relate to AI, explores real-world applications, and develops learners' ability to evaluate AI-generated information responsibly.

The presentation uses accessible language, practical examples, and a short critical-thinking activity to support understanding rather than memorisation alone.

## 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of the presentation, learners should be able to:

1. Define artificial intelligence in their own words.
2. Distinguish AI, machine learning, and generative AI.
3. Identify at least three applications of AI in everyday life.
4. Explain two limitations or risks associated with AI systems.
5. Apply basic checks to evaluate AI-generated information.
6. Identify responsible practices for using AI, including privacy protection, human oversight, and verification of important claims.

### Slide 3 — What Is Artificial Intelligence?

**On-slide content**
- Artificial intelligence refers to machine-based systems that infer from inputs to generate outputs, such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions.
- AI systems can perform tasks associated with human abilities, including recognising patterns, processing language, and supporting problem-solving.
- AI capabilities differ across systems and tasks.

**Example:** A navigation application estimates travel times and recommends routes using available data.

**Visual suggestion:** A simple input → AI system → output diagram.

**Speaker notes:**  
Explain AI using familiar examples. Clarify that AI is not a single tool or one specific technology. Different systems are built for different purposes and have different strengths and limitations.

**Estimated time:** 50 seconds

### Slide 4 — AI, Machine Learning, and Generative AI

**On-slide content**
- **Artificial intelligence (AI):** The broad field of building systems that perform tasks requiring capabilities such as prediction, reasoning, perception, or language processing.
- **Machine learning (ML):** A subset of AI in which systems learn patterns from data to make predictions or produce outputs.
- **Generative AI:** AI that generates new content, such as text, images, audio, or code.

**Example**
- AI: A system that helps detect unusual activity.
- ML: A model trained on data to identify patterns in transactions.
- Generative AI: A tool that drafts a summary of a document.

**Visual suggestion:** A nested-circle diagram showing machine learning within AI and generative AI as a category of AI methods that can overlap with machine learning.

**Speaker notes:**  
Explain that these terms are related but not interchangeable. Machine learning is one approach within AI, while generative AI describes systems that create content. Avoid suggesting that every AI system uses machine learning or that all AI generates content.

**Estimated time:** 55 seconds

### Slide 5 — How Does Machine Learning Work?

**On-slide content**
A simplified machine-learning workflow:
1. Collect relevant data.
2. Train a model to learn patterns.
3. Evaluate the model using suitable data and measures.
4. Use the model to generate predictions or outputs.
5. Monitor performance and improve the system when needed.

**Example:** A spam filter learns patterns associated with unwanted messages and classifies incoming emails.

**Key reminder:** Data quality affects model performance.

**Visual suggestion:** A five-step process diagram.

**Speaker notes:**  
Explain that machine learning does not mean a system understands the world exactly as a person does. Models learn patterns from data, and poor-quality or unrepresentative data can contribute to errors or unfair outcomes.

**Estimated time:** 50 seconds

### Slide 6 — What Is Generative AI?

**On-slide content**
- Generates content in response to a prompt or other input.
- Can assist with brainstorming, summarising, drafting, coding, and creating images.
- May produce inaccurate, misleading, biased, or fabricated information.
- Requires human review when accuracy matters.

**Example:** A learner asks a generative AI tool to explain a difficult concept, then checks the explanation against reliable learning materials.

**Visual suggestion:** Prompt → AI-generated draft → human verification.

**Speaker notes:**  
Explain that generative AI can support learning and creativity, but a fluent response is not necessarily a correct response. Encourage learners to treat outputs as material to evaluate, not as automatically verified facts.

**Estimated time:** 50 seconds

### Slide 7 — AI in Everyday Life

**On-slide content**
Examples of AI applications include:
- **Education:** Personalised practice and learning support.
- **Healthcare:** Assistance with selected image-analysis and administrative tasks.
- **Transport:** Route estimation and traffic prediction.
- **Accessibility:** Speech recognition and text-to-speech tools.
- **Communication:** Spam filtering, translation, and writing assistance.

**Key message:** AI can support people in different contexts, but suitability depends on the task and system.

**Visual suggestion:** Five clearly labelled icons, one for each application area.

**Speaker notes:**  
Use familiar examples to show the range of AI applications. Explain that an AI system's presence in a field does not mean it can safely or accurately perform every task in that field. High-impact decisions need appropriate oversight.

**Estimated time:** 50 seconds

### Slide 8 — Benefits and Limitations of AI

**On-slide content**

**Potential benefits**
- Automates some repetitive tasks.
- Processes large amounts of information.
- Supports accessibility and productivity.
- Helps people explore ideas and patterns.

**Limitations and risks**
- Can produce errors or unreliable predictions.
- Can reflect bias in data or design.
- May expose sensitive information if used carelessly.
- Can make mistakes that are difficult to detect.
- May be unsuitable for some high-stakes decisions without safeguards.

**Visual suggestion:** A balanced two-column comparison.

**Speaker notes:**  
Emphasise that benefits and risks can exist at the same time. Whether AI is appropriate depends on its design, the quality of its evidence, the context of use, and the consequences of errors.

**Estimated time:** 55 seconds

### Slide 9 — Responsible and Ethical AI Use

**On-slide content**
Responsible AI use includes:
- **Accuracy:** Verify important claims with reliable sources.
- **Privacy:** Avoid entering confidential or sensitive information into tools without appropriate safeguards.
- **Fairness:** Consider whether outputs could disadvantage particular groups.
- **Transparency:** Be clear about when AI has materially contributed to work when disclosure is required or appropriate.
- **Human oversight:** Keep people involved in consequential decisions.
- **Accountability:** Follow relevant institutional rules and take responsibility for submitted work.

**Visual suggestion:** Six clearly labelled principles arranged around a central human-oversight symbol.

**Speaker notes:**  
Explain that responsible use involves more than avoiding mistakes. It also includes protecting information, considering fairness, being transparent, and following the rules of the learning or working environment. AI should support human judgement, not replace responsibility.

**Estimated time:** 60 seconds

### Slide 10 — Critical-Thinking Activity: Can We Trust This Claim?

**On-slide content**

Consider this statement:

> “Research proves that using AI improves every student's academic performance.”

Discuss:
1. What evidence would be needed to support this statement?
2. Is the word “every” justified?
3. How could you verify the claim using reliable sources?
4. How might the effects of AI differ between students or learning tasks?

**Suggested approach**
- Identify the claim.
- Look for credible evidence.
- Check the date, context, and research method.
- Compare more than one reliable source.
- Decide whether the evidence supports the wording.

**Visual suggestion:** A claim card beside a magnifying glass and evidence checklist.

**Speaker notes:**  
Give learners a short pause to think or discuss in pairs. Explain that the statement is deliberately broad. A responsible evaluation would require evidence from relevant research, attention to differences between contexts, and careful consideration of the word “every.” Do not present the statement as an established fact.

**Estimated time:** 75 seconds

### Slide 11 — Knowledge Check and Key Takeaways

**On-slide content**

**Quick questions**
1. How is machine learning related to AI?
2. Name one possible benefit and one limitation of generative AI.
3. What should you do before relying on an important AI-generated claim?
4. Name one practice that helps protect privacy when using AI.

**Key takeaways**
- AI is a broad field with many different applications.
- Machine learning is one approach within AI.
- Generative AI can create content but may make mistakes.
- Verification, privacy, fairness, and human oversight matter.

**Visual suggestion:** Four quiz icons followed by a concise takeaway checklist.

**Speaker notes:**  
Allow learners to answer the questions before reviewing the key takeaways. Reinforce the learning objectives and correct misunderstandings respectfully. Learners should be able to explain the concepts in their own words.

**Estimated time:** 60 seconds

- Use relevant visuals that clarify concepts rather than merely decorate slides.

- Check spelling, grammar, layout consistency, and readability before submission.
- Use only images, icons, and other assets that the team has permission to use; record sources and licences as required.
- Ensure the final presentation remains understandable without assuming advanced AI knowledge.
